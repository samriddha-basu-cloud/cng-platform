CNG Supply Chain Network Design & Scarcity Simulation Platform

A decision-support system for designing Compressed Natural Gas distribution networks that stay cost-effective under normal operation and stay functional — not just cheap — when gas supply is uncertain or rationed.

Facility Location Mixed-Integer Linear Programming Two-Stage Stochastic Optimization Scarcity & Resilience Analysis
Open CNG Network Design & Scarcity Platform

What problem does this answer?

Given a configurable CNG supply network — gas sources, City Gate Stations (CGS), retail CNG stations, and demand zones — uncertain gas availability, and service-priority requirements, what network configuration and gas allocation strategy minimizes total cost while maintaining acceptable service levels, under both normal and scarcity conditions?

The model, in one line

The full formulation lives on the Framework tab; the shape of it is this —

minimize   Infrastructure + Supply + Transport + Priority-weighted Shortage subject to capacity, flow-balance & service-level constraints

Who this is for

The platform is built to be read two ways at once. An academic reader can inspect the exact mathematical formulation, the solver used, and the assumptions behind every number — see the Framework and Notes on Results tabs. An industry reader — a planner at a city gas distribution company, or someone scoping infrastructure investment — can ignore the mathematics entirely and work from cost breakdowns, utilization figures, and plain-language recommendations. Neither reading requires touching the other.

What's actually running underneath

This is not a static mock-up or a slide deck pretending to be a tool. Every number shown anywhere in the app — cost, utilization, resilience score, recommendation — comes from solving a real Mixed-Integer Linear Program against whatever network you've built, using the HiGHS open-source solver via Pyomo, with CBC, Gurobi, and CPLEX supported as pluggable alternatives (see Framework). Nothing is hard-coded to the demo network sizes; the same code path handles a 3-source network and a 50-source network identically.

The gap this project addresses

Classic facility-location and supply-chain network design models — the textbook p-median or capacitated facility location problem — optimize for cost under the standing assumption that supply is reliably available whenever a facility calls for it. That assumption fits manufacturing and retail logistics reasonably well. It does not fit gas distribution in a country where domestic production is politically allocated (APM cuts), LNG import prices spike with global markets, and winter heating demand competes directly with transport CNG demand for the same pipeline capacity.

Most published CNG/CGD network studies either (a) treat capacity as fixed and optimize location and flow under certainty, or (b) model scarcity qualitatively, as a narrative risk section, without folding it into the optimization itself. Few make scarcity the organizing variable of the model — where the facility-location decision and the rationing policy are solved jointly, and where "who loses service first" is a designed output of a priority-weighted objective rather than an afterthought.

Three specific shortfalls in existing practice

First, facility location and rationing policy are usually solved sequentially, not jointly — a network is designed for average or peak-certain demand, and a curtailment policy is layered on afterward as an operational rule rather than a variable the design itself accounts for. Second, service priority is rarely made quantitative. Regulatory and commercial guidance on who should be served first during a shortfall — public transport before private vehicles, for instance — usually exists as policy language, not as a weighted term inside an optimization that can trade it off against cost explicitly. Third, infeasibility is treated as a modeling failure to avoid, not a planning signal to interpret. A network design study that returns "no feasible solution" under a stress scenario has actually told the planner something important — that the current infrastructure cannot meet its minimum service commitments under that shock — but most tooling either prevents that outcome by construction (soft-capping everything) or reports it as an error rather than a diagnosis.

The working assumption behind that gap

The core assumption motivating this project: a network designed to be cost-optimal under 100% supply availability is not the same network that is cost-optimal — or even feasible — under realistic scarcity. A network sized and routed assuming full supply may have no redundancy to fall back on when a single source or corridor underperforms, while a network with deliberate slack costs more in the common case to survive the uncommon one. The right answer is neither extreme, and finding it requires solving the scarcity case explicitly rather than bolting a safety margin onto a certainty-case answer after the fact. This is why the platform treats deterministic, scenario-based, two-stage stochastic, and robust optimization as four different lenses on the same underlying network — not four unrelated features.

Modeling assumptions

Every assumption below is a deliberate simplification, stated so it can be argued with rather than discovered by surprise.

  • Static per-period demand within a solve. A single deterministic or scenario solve treats demand as a fixed number for that period; growth across periods is handled by the separate time-series simulation, not inside one MILP solve.
  • Linear costs, no economies of scale. Transport and supply costs are linear in flow — there's no volume discount or congestion penalty modeled on any arc.
  • CGS→station flow has no arc-level capacity or explicit cost entity. Only facility (node) capacities constrain that hop; its cost is distance-based (haversine) or a flat default, not a user-entered transport contract.
  • Priority-weighted shortage penalty, not a hard allocation rule. Service priority is enforced by (a) a per-zone minimum service level constraint and (b) a penalty weight on unmet demand in the objective — the model chooses where to let shortage fall based on cost trade-offs within those bounds, it does not follow a fixed waterfall.
  • Two-stage stochastic assumes fixed topology across scenarios. Facility open/close decisions are shared "here-and-now" variables; only flows, costs, and availability vary by scenario. A scenario cannot introduce a facility that doesn't exist in the base network.
  • Virtual pipeline (M-D-O) is a labeled corridor, not a separate mode-choice optimization. The platform lets you tag a corridor as "virtual," but it does not yet independently decide pipeline vs. virtual pipeline economics.
  • No inventory carried between simulation periods. The multi-period timeline is a sequence of independent solves with compounding demand growth, not a single formulation with a storage/inventory balance variable across time.

The full, code-linked version of this list — including the exact constraint each assumption maps to — is in MODEL_DOCUMENTATION.md in the project repository.

Project objectives

  1. Formulate CNG network design as a capacitated facility-location MILP that is generic over network size — no part of the optimization logic references a specific count of sources, corridors, CGS, stations, or demand zones.
  2. Incorporate scarcity as a first-class modeling dimension, not an afterthought: source availability factors, scenario-based and two-stage stochastic formulations, and a configurable robust mode.
  3. Encode service priority explicitly, so that when supply falls short, which demand goes unmet is a transparent, priority-weighted output of the model rather than an implicit side effect.
  4. Make infeasibility explainable. When a scenario cannot be served, the system should say why — insufficient aggregate supply, a disconnected demand zone, an unreachable minimum service level — instead of returning a bare solver failure.
  5. Quantify resilience with a transparent, decomposable score rather than a single opaque number, so a user can see which lever (diversification, slack, redundancy, continuity) is actually driving the result.
  6. Present the same underlying results in two registers: a technical, formulation-level view for research/coursework use, and a plain-language, cost-and-risk view for operational decision-making.

Alignment with the UN Sustainable Development Goals

These objectives were not chosen to satisfy an SDG checklist after the fact — but the connection is direct enough to state explicitly, since it's part of why CNG network resilience is a policy-relevant problem and not only a cost problem.

SDG 7
Affordable and Clean Energy

CNG is a lower-emission bridge fuel relative to diesel/petrol for transport and industrial use. A network that keeps supply reliable, not just cheap, is a precondition for CNG remaining a credible substitute rather than a fuel people fall back off during shortages.

SDG 9
Industry, Innovation & Infrastructure

The core contribution is an infrastructure-investment decision tool — where to add CGS or station capacity, and how much redundancy is worth paying for — aimed directly at building resilient distribution infrastructure.

SDG 11
Sustainable Cities & Communities

The priority-class mechanism is built to protect public transport service levels first during shortages — the segment with the largest per-unit impact on urban air quality and commuter access.

SDG 12
Responsible Consumption & Production

Modeling scarcity explicitly, rather than over-provisioning "just in case," is itself a resource-efficiency argument — the platform's Pareto-frontier analysis quantifies exactly how much slack a given service-level target actually requires, rather than defaulting to worst-case overbuild.

SDG 13
Climate Action

Every unit of CNG demand that goes unmet during a shortage is demand that may shift back to a higher-emission substitute fuel in the near term. Keeping the network resilient under scarcity is, indirectly, an emissions-stability argument as much as a service-continuity one.

What this platform claims — and what it doesn't

Claims made

  • The optimization is a genuine solver run (HiGHS via Pyomo) against whatever network the user configures — not a canned or interpolated result.
  • Facility counts, arc counts, and constraint counts scale with the actual network size; a hand-computed known-optimal test case (tests/test_optimization.py::test_known_optimal_value) is asserted to match the solver's output exactly, as a correctness check.
  • The resilience score's components and weights are inspectable and reconfigurable at request time — it is not a fixed, undocumented formula.
  • Every recommendation the platform surfaces is generated from a rule evaluated against real solve output (utilization, concentration, unmet demand) — never a templated or fabricated statement.

Claims explicitly NOT made

  • This is not validated against real GAIL/IGL/MGL network data. Every number in the demo networks is illustrative and labeled as such throughout the interface, exports, and reports.
  • The two-stage stochastic and robust modes are working implementations of standard formulations, not claims of novel optimization research.
  • Infeasibility diagnostics are a heuristic screen against the common causes, not a formal IIS (irreducible infeasible subsystem) computation.
  • The platform does not claim production-scale performance guarantees at the largest instance sizes described in its own design brief (e.g. 20 sources × 50 CGS × 100 stations × 100 demand zones) — see Notes on Results.

Why this matters to a City Gas Distribution operator

India's CGD sector operates under the Administered Price Mechanism, where domestic gas allocation to CNG/PNG can be curtailed to prioritize other sectors during shortfalls, and where LNG spot-price volatility directly affects the economics of make-up supply. A network built purely for lowest cost under normal supply can find itself either paying an emergency premium for spot LNG during a shortfall, or — worse — simply unable to serve committed public transport contracts, which typically carry regulatory and reputational consequences beyond the immediate lost revenue.

Concrete decisions this platform is built to support

  • Where to add infrastructure. The Analyze tab's recommendation engine flags CGS/stations running near capacity under stress scenarios — candidates for expansion — using real utilization figures, not a rule of thumb.
  • How much redundancy is worth paying for. The cost-vs-service-level Pareto frontier makes the trade-off explicit: what does each additional percentage point of guaranteed service level actually cost, and where does the curve bend.
  • Which supply contracts create concentration risk. The resilience score's supply-diversification component and the recommendation engine's source-concentration check both surface over-reliance on a single source.
  • What a specific shock would do to the network today. The one-click scenario presets (20/40/60% shortage, demand surge, pipeline failure, combined crisis) give a before/after comparison in seconds, without needing to hand-build a scenario.

Positioning relative to existing tools

This is closer to an operations-research decision-support prototype than to a SCADA/telemetry system or a GIS asset register — it doesn't ingest live operational data or control physical infrastructure. It sits upstream of those systems, in the planning and investment-justification stage, where the question is "what should the network look like," not "what is the network doing right now."

Network topology

Four echelons. Nothing about entity counts is fixed — the diagram below describes the shape of the flow, not any particular network's size.

Decision variables

  • yj ∈ {0,1} — 1 if CGS j is open (fixed to 1 if existing)
  • zk ∈ {0,1} — 1 if station k is open (fixed to 1 if existing)
  • xsj, wjk, vkd ≥ 0 — flow on each arc
  • ud ≥ 0 — unmet demand at zone d

Objective

Minimize the sum of six cost components below, subject to the constraints that follow. Every subscript names the set it ranges over — j ∈ J (CGS), k ∈ K (stations), d ∈ D (demand zones), and (s,j), (j,k), (k,d) for arcs between them.

minimize   Σj∈J FixedCostj · yj CGS fixed operating cost
+   Σk∈K FixedCostk · zk station fixed cost
+   Σ(s,j) SupplyCosts · xsj gas procurement cost
+   Σ(s,j) TransportCostsj · xsj source → CGS transport
+   Σ(j,k) DistanceCostjk · wjk CGS → station transport
+   Σ(k,d) DistanceCostkd · vkd station → demand distribution
+   Σd∈D PenaltyBase · PriorityWeightd · ud priority-weighted shortage penalty

Core constraints

  • Source capacity   Σj xsj ≤ Availabilitys · MaxCapacitys
  • Corridor capacity   xsj ≤ Capacitysj
  • Flow balance   at every CGS and station: inbound = outbound
  • Facility capacity   inbound flow ≤ Capacity · (yj or zk)
  • Demand balance   vkd (delivered) + ud (unmet) = Demandd
  • Service level   ud ≤ (1 − MinServiceLeveld) · Demandd

Solvers

The optimization engine is written against Pyomo's solver-agnostic API (app/optimization/model_builder.py builds one ConcreteModel; engine.py solves it and reads results back off the variables) — the formulation above is identical no matter which solver executes it, and nothing in the model, the API, or the UI changes based on which one is active. Only solve speed and, on very large instances, numerical robustness differ. Four solvers are supported, checked in this preference order at runtime — app/optimization/solver_registry.py is the single place that knows what's actually usable in the current environment, and it reports whichever it finds first as the "Active Solver" throughout the app:

1. HiGHS — bundled, default 2. CBC — open-source, auto-provisioned 3. Gurobi — commercial, license required 4. CPLEX — commercial, license required

HiGHS is a modern open-source MILP/LP solver, used via Pyomo's appsi persistent interface and installed automatically through the highspy PyPI package — no system install, no PATH setup, no license. It's the default because it's fast, actively maintained, and needs nothing beyond pip install -r requirements.txt.

CBC (COIN-OR Branch and Cut) is the other fully open-source option, and it's auto-provisioned the same way: the registry first checks for a cbc binary on PATH (a system package like brew install cbc or apt install coinor-cbc), and if none is found, falls back to the CBC binary that ships inside the pulp PyPI package for the current OS/architecture (macOS, Linux x86_64/arm64, Windows are all covered) — so pip install pulp is enough to bring CBC online with zero manual solver installation either. It runs as a second, independent implementation of the same MILP — useful as a cross-check against HiGHS on results that matter, since two different solver codebases agreeing on an optimum is stronger evidence than one solver's word for it.

Gurobi and CPLEX are commercial, closed-source solvers from Gurobi Optimization and IBM respectively. They genuinely cannot be auto-installed or bundled the way HiGHS/CBC are — each requires its own separate installer and a paid, machine- or user-tied license file that only the license holder can obtain (both do offer free academic licenses). The registry detects them via Pyomo's SolverFactory exactly like CBC, and the app will pick one up and use it automatically the moment it's installed and licensed on the host machine — nothing else needs to change. Their presence here reflects that enterprise deployments that already hold a license can point this platform at them without touching the model code; they are not, and were never meant to be, a requirement to run anything on this page.

Technical architecture

Flask Pyomo Pyomo appsi NetworkX JSON file storage (no database) Leaflet.js Chart.js Vanilla JS openpyxl / reportlab (export)

Storage is a small repository layer over plain JSON files (app/data/repo.py) rather than a SQL database — every project is one file, every optimization run appends one line to a log, and there is no ORM or migration system in the stack.

How to read any result this platform produces

Every cost, utilization figure, and recommendation is real solver output against the network as configured at the moment you ran it — but "real" does not mean "validated against actual industry data." Read every number through two filters before acting on it:

  1. Is the underlying data illustrative or real? Check the project's is_demo flag and the per-value assumption ledger. Demo network numbers are placeholders for exploring the tool, not benchmarks.
  2. Which modeling simplification does this result brush up against? See the Assumptions tab. A recommendation to expand a CGS, for instance, is only as good as the fixed/linear cost assumption behind the expansion-cost figure it's computed from.

Known limitations, stated plainly

  • No inter-period inventory/storage state — the time-series simulation is a sequence of independent solves, not one multi-period MILP.
  • CGS→station arcs have no explicit corridor entity or arc-level capacity.
  • Virtual pipeline / M-D-O mode choice is a label, not an independent optimization decision between pipeline and virtual-pipeline economics.
  • No direct budget-constrained investment mode ("minimum investment for 95% service") — approximate it via the Pareto frontier instead.
  • Analytics (Pareto, tornado, resilience, dashboard summary) re-solve the model multiple times per request rather than caching — fine at demo scale, untested at the large-instance target described in the platform's own design brief (20 sources × 50 CGS × 100 stations × 100 demand zones).
  • Infeasibility diagnostics are a heuristic screen against common causes, not a formal IIS/conflict-refiner computation.

What has and hasn't been verified

Verified: 27 automated tests cover CRUD/validation correctness, a hand-computed known-optimal MILP case matched exactly, infeasibility diagnostics against genuinely infeasible instances, stochastic and robust solving, and several analytics invariants (Pareto monotonicity, tornado sort order). Both demo networks solve correctly end to end, including the advanced network's deliberately infeasible outage scenario being correctly diagnosed rather than silently mishandled.

Not verified: performance and correctness at large instance sizes; behavior with pathological inputs (extremely tight service levels combined with disconnected sub-networks, for instance, has only been spot-checked, not systematically fuzzed); and — the most important caveat — none of this has been checked against real-world CGD network data, because none was used to build it.
